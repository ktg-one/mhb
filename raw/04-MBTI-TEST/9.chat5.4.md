I’m sorry. Getting fired over something like that is brutal.

The short answer is:

**You should have treated AI as a drafting/tooling assistant, not as a trusted source of facts.**  
For anything going to a board, client, regulator, or executive team, **every important claim, number, and citation needed to be independently verified from primary sources** before you presented it.

What that means in practice:

### What you should have done differently

1. **Never present AI-generated facts without checking them**
	- If AI gave you numbers, market data, customer stats, legal claims, citations, or trends, you needed to verify each one.
		- AI can sound confident while being wrong.
2. **Use primary sources for board-level material**
	- Internal dashboards
		- Finance reports
		- CRM / ERP exports
		- Official vendor data
		- Public filings / audited statements
		- Original research reports, not AI summaries of them
3. **Require proof for every key number**  
	Before a board presentation, every important figure should answer:
	- Where did this number come from?
		- Who owns that data?
		- When was it last updated?
		- Can I reproduce it?
4. **Treat citations from AI as untrusted until inspected**
	- AI sometimes invents sources or misstates real ones.
		- You should have opened the actual source documents yourself.
5. **Separate “help writing” from “help thinking” from “help proving”**  
	AI is much safer for:
	- drafting slides
		- summarizing notes you already verified
		- suggesting questions
		- improving wording
	It is much riskier for:
	- generating market data
		- estimating financials
		- interpreting ambiguous evidence
		- producing board-ready conclusions
6. **Do a human review before high-stakes presentations**  
	Ideally:
	- Finance reviews financial claims
		- Ops reviews operational claims
		- Data/analytics reviews methodology
		- Legal/compliance reviews sensitive claims
		- Your manager signs off
7. **Recompute critical numbers yourself**  
	If a figure mattered to the board’s decision, you should have rebuilt it:
	- in a spreadsheet
		- from source exports
		- or through validated internal reporting
8. **Be explicit about uncertainty**  
	If the data was incomplete, the right move was:
	- “This is directional, not confirmed”
		- “We need another 48 hours to validate”
		- “Here are the assumptions”
	That is always safer than presenting uncertain output as fact.
9. **Keep an audit trail**  
	For each major slide:
	- source
		- date
		- owner
		- assumptions
		- calculation method
	If you can’t defend a slide line-by-line, it shouldn’t go to the board.
10. **Push back on speed if necessary**  
	If you were under time pressure, the better move was:
- delay the meeting,
- narrow the scope,
- or clearly mark items as pending validation.

A late accurate deck is better than a polished wrong one.

---

## A simple rule for the future

For any high-stakes use of AI:

### Use AI to:

- brainstorm
- outline
- draft
- summarize verified material
- generate questions
- improve communication

### Don’t use AI alone to:

- supply facts
- produce final numbers
- create citations
- make legal/financial/compliance claims
- support decisions without human validation
