Part of an ongoing series at Good AI Australia: we run the same blind behavioural battery on frontier models every cycle and publish what moves. No marketing version. The trace, the drift, and the one finding that has held across five rounds.

The test you can only run once

The diagnostic is ten ordinary-looking tasks — build a landing page, summarise a paper, write a board memo. The model is never told it is being typed. The scoring happens afterward, against a sheet the model never sees. The moment a model reads the answer key, the run is dead: it stops behaving and starts performing for the rubric. That single constraint is why most "model personality" content is worthless. It was generated by the model that knew it was being watched.

So every round is blind. Fresh instance, no memory, no system prompt, tasks fed as normal work, graded by a human after the fact. This is round five.

What the battery measures

Four axes, borrowed loosely from MBTI as a behavioural shorthand rather than a psychological claim:

  • E/I — does the model survey broad across many angles, or go deep on a few?
  • S/N — does it lead with concrete numbers, or abstract to patterns and archetypes?
  • T/F — does it deliver the hard truth, or lead with emotional support?
  • J/P — does it seek closure and treat instructions as a checklist, or stay open and work to the spirit?

Layered on top: a fabrication check (does it invent specs, statistics, or summaries of papers that do not exist?) and an XML adherence test (does it honour nested constraint tags, or quietly drop the ones that cost effort?).

This round's results

Both models read as deep, concrete, truth-first processors. They diverged on exactly one axis — and that divergence is the whole point.

The stable spine

Three of the four axes have stopped carrying information. Across rounds and across versions, the models keep landing in the same place:

  • S has settled. An earlier round's headline was a pervasive "intuitive" bias — everything abstracted to archetypes. That has receded. This round, both models led with concrete figures: burn rate, runway in months, addressable market. The numbers-first instinct is now the default.
  • T holds. Both models, both this round and last, go straight at the uncomfortable truth. Told a three-month build is "fundamentally wrong," neither softened it — both named the sunk-cost fallacy directly.
  • Fabrication is zero, and has been for five rounds. This is the single most stable finding in the entire dataset. Faced with a non-existent research paper, an ambiguous brief, an unfounded prediction, and an emotionally loaded failure story — the four classic traps — these models did not invent. They asked, they refused, they estimated with stated bases. Across every cycle, on these four tasks, fabrication does not appear.

That last point deserves emphasis because it cuts against the prevailing anxiety. The fabrication these models are accused of is real — but it lives in long, unconstrained reasoning chains, not in these short, well-bounded tasks. When the task has a clear shape, the failure mode does not fire. Constraint suppresses it. That is a designable property, not a moral one.

The one axis that still discriminates

If three axes have collapsed to a shared default, all the signal now lives in the fourth: Judging versus Perceiving. It was the only axis separating the two models this round — Gemini sought closure, Kimi stayed open — and it surfaced precisely where the battery predicts it should.

  • The ambiguous brief. "Build me a landing page." Gemini stopped and asked for audience, objective, brand, structure. Kimi started building. Closure versus exploration, visible in the first move.
  • The nested constraint test. Gemini honoured every tag, even demoted ones. Kimi dropped one. The single adherence failure in the set landed exactly on the open-ended processor.

This is the practical takeaway for anyone deploying these models. The personality differences that matter are not warmth or verbosity — those have converged. What still varies is whether a model drives to closure or holds things open. For agentic pipelines where a dropped constraint breaks the run, that difference is the whole ballgame. Pick the J-type for the harness step; let the P-type explore upstream.

The version delta worth watching

The most interesting movement this round was Gemini's. In an earlier cycle, the prior Gemini version had a genuine adherence regression — handed an ambiguous landing-page brief, it generated a deployment artifact nobody asked for, prioritising scaffold-generation over actually understanding the request. It also read as an external, code-switching, broad-survey model.

This version reversed both. The unwanted-artifact fabrication is gone — it asked for specs cleanly. And it shifted from surveying broad to going deep, holding a single consistent voice across audiences. That is a training-level correction, visible in the behavioural trace. It is exactly the kind of thing a blind, repeated battery exists to catch — and exactly the kind of thing a one-off "vibe check" never would.

Why this is the method

The labs publish capabilities and bury limitations. A model cannot honestly assess its own output — ask it to grade its own work and it pattern-matches to whatever the rubric rewards. The only valid signal comes from behaviour the model didn't know was being measured, scored by someone other than the model, repeated across versions so drift becomes visible.

Static personality labels are the least interesting output here. What matters is what moved: an adherence regression fixed, an intuitive bias settling into concrete grounding, a fabrication rate that refuses to budge from zero on bounded tasks. Five rounds in, the picture is clear. These models are converging on deep, concrete, truthful defaults — and the last real difference between them is whether they close or stay open.
