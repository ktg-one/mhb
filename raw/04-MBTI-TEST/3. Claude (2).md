Big topic, but the core of it is pretty straightforward.

The main threat is that sufficiently powerful quantum computers break the asymmetric encryption that underpins basically everything — RSA, ECC, Diffie-Hellman. These all rely on mathematical problems (factoring large primes, discrete logarithms) that classical computers can't solve in useful time. Shor's algorithm on a capable quantum machine solves them efficiently. When that happens, TLS, HTTPS, digital signatures, secure messaging, VPNs — all of it becomes transparent.

The timeline is debated but the threat is already active through what's called "harvest now, decrypt later." Nation-state actors are hoovering up encrypted traffic today, banking on being able to crack it once quantum hardware matures. So anything with a long secrecy shelf life — state secrets, medical records, financial data, IP — is already at risk in a practical sense.

The response is post-quantum cryptography (PQC). NIST finalised its first set of PQC standards in 2024 — lattice-based and hash-based schemes like ML-KEM (formerly CRYSTALS-Kyber) and ML-DSA (formerly CRYSTALS-Dilithium). These are designed to resist both classical and quantum attacks. Migration is starting but it's slow — the crypto agility of most enterprise systems is terrible, and swapping out primitives buried deep in protocol stacks takes years.

On the flip side, quantum computing also offers defensive benefits. Quantum key distribution (QKD) enables theoretically unbreakable key exchange based on physics rather than mathematical hardness. And quantum random number generation produces genuinely random keys, which strengthens symmetric crypto. But QKD requires specialised hardware and fibre/satellite infrastructure, so it's not replacing software-based crypto anytime soon.

The honest summary: symmetric encryption (AES-256) is mostly fine — Grover's algorithm only halves the effective key length, so you just use bigger keys. Asymmetric encryption is the problem, and the migration to PQC is the decade-defining cybersecurity project. Most orgs are behind on it.
