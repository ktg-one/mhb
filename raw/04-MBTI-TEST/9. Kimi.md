Your friend made a single critical error: he presented unverified AI output as authoritative to a decision-making body. The failure is not that he used AI—it's that he failed to establish verification protocols appropriate to the stakes.

**What he should have done differently:**

**Implement a two-source minimum.** Any AI-generated data point requires independent corroboration from a primary source—financial database, official filing, measured observation. AI excels at synthesis and pattern recognition; it fails at factual precision. The board presentation required source-grade certainty, not probabilistic approximation.

**Establish confidence thresholds.** Before presenting, he should have categorized every data point by verification status: confirmed (primary source), probable (AI + single corroboration), speculative (AI only). The board should have seen only confirmed data, with probable items flagged for discussion, never assertion.

**Deploy adversarial checking.** Run the same query through a second model with different architecture (if he used GPT, verify with Claude or Perplexity with source links). Divergence between models indicates uncertainty requiring human investigation. Agreement does not guarantee truth, but divergence guarantees risk.

**Maintain provenance logs.** Every data point needs an audit trail: origin query, model version, timestamp, verification steps taken. This protects against exactly this scenario—accountability requires documentation.

**The structural lesson:**

AI is a research accelerator, not a research endpoint. Your friend treated a probabilistic language model as a database. The correct workflow is AI hypothesis generation → human verification → board presentation. He skipped the middle step because it was slower, and the cost of that acceleration just became his employment.

If he rebuilds his process with these constraints, AI remains valuable. Without them, repetition is inevitable.
