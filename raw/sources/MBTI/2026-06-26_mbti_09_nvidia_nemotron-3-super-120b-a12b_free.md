### ✅ What You Should Have Done Differently

#### 1. **Treat AI Output as a Draft, Not a Gospel**
- AI hallucinates. It confidently invents citations, misinterprets context, and pulls from outdated or biased data.
- **Action:** Always ask: *“Where did this come from?”*  
  → Demand sources. If AI can’t provide verifiable, traceable origins (e.g., peer-reviewed papers, official reports, internal databases), treat it as unverified speculation.

#### 2. **Implement a Human-in-the-Loop Validation Process**
- Before presenting anything to leadership — especially the board — run AI-generated content through:
  - A subject-matter expert (SME)
  - A data analyst or researcher
  - A checklist: *Is this plausible? Does it match known trends? Can I replicate the logic?*
- Think of AI as your research assistant — not your decision-maker.

#### 3. **Document Your Process (CYA — Cover Your Ass)**
- Keep a log:  
  - *What prompt did I use?*  
  - *What version of the AI model?*  
  - *What did I verify? What did I skip?*  
  - *Who reviewed it?*  
- This isn’t just bureaucracy — it’s your professional shield. If something goes wrong, you can show you exercised due diligence.

#### 4. **Communicate Uncertainty Transparently**
- If you *must* use AI-generated insights, frame them honestly:  
  > “Preliminary analysis from our AI tool suggests X trend — however, this requires validation against our internal sales data and market reports. I’ve initiated a verification step with the analytics team and will have confirmed results by [date].”  
- This shows intelligence, humility, and rigor — not naivety.

#### 5. **Push Back on AI-Overreliance Culture**
- If your team or leadership encouraged blind trust in AI, that’s a cultural failure — not just yours.  
- Use this experience to advocate for better AI governance:  
  - Request training on AI limitations  
  - Propose an AI use policy (e.g., “No AI-generated data in executive presentations without SME sign-off”)  
  - Become the person who helps others avoid this mistake.
