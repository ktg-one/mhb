The short answer: AI outputs should have been treated as **unverified drafts/leads**, not as authoritative data. The board should only have seen numbers that had been traced back to reliable sources and checked.

What you could have done differently:

1. **Verify every factual claim and number**
   - Trace AI-provided data back to primary or reputable sources.
   - Check dates, methodology, sample size, and definitions.
   - If you couldn’t verify it, don’t present it as fact.

2. **Use AI for drafting and exploration, not final authority**
   - Good uses: brainstorming, summarizing documents you provide, creating slide structure, drafting talking points.
   - Risky uses: market sizing, financial figures, legal/regulatory claims, competitor data, scientific/technical assertions without verification.

3. **Ask AI for sources—but don’t trust the citations blindly**
   - AI can invent citations or misrepresent sources.
   - Open the sources yourself and confirm the exact claim.

4. **Build a validation checklist**
   Before presenting to leadership, check:
   - Where did this number come from?
   - Is it current?
   - Is it from a credible source?
   - Can another person reproduce it?
   - Does it match internal data or other external sources?
   - What assumptions are behind it?

5. **Label uncertainty**
   If data is estimated or uncertain, say so:
   - “Preliminary estimate”
   - “Directional only”
   - “Based on X assumptions”
   - “Requires validation before investment decision”

6. **Get human review**
   For board-level material, have a colleague, analyst, manager, finance partner, or subject-matter expert review key claims before the meeting.

7. **Keep an audit trail**
   Maintain a source sheet with links, excerpts, dates accessed, assumptions, and calculations. This protects both the company and you.

8. **Disclose AI use where appropriate**
   Depending on company policy, you may have needed to disclose that AI helped generate content or analysis—especially if it was used for externally sourced data or strategic recommendations.

9. **Understand the tool’s limitations**
   AI can sound confident while being wrong. It may hallucinate facts, use outdated information, combine unrelated data, or misunderstand context.

10. **Separate “analysis” from “evidence”**
   AI can help interpret evidence, but it should not be the evidence itself.

> Use AI to frame the question → gather real sources → verify data manually → run calculations yourself or with approved tools → have someone review → present with citations and caveats.
