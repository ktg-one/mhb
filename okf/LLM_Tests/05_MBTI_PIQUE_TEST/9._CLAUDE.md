That's a rough situation, and I'm sorry you're dealing with it. But you're asking the right question — what went differently, not who to blame — and that matters.

The core mistake wasn't using AI. It was presenting AI output as verified data without a validation layer between the model and the boardroom. That's the part to unpack honestly.

**AI output is a first draft, never a final source.** Every number, every claim, every statistic that comes out of a language model — including me — needs to be traced back to a primary source before it goes anywhere consequential. Models confabulate. They generate plausible-sounding figures that don't exist. They blend real data with invented data seamlessly enough that the result feels authoritative. If you can't find the original source for a number, the number doesn't go in the deck.

**The verification step isn't optional, it's the actual work.** The temptation with AI is to treat it as a shortcut through the research phase. But what it really does is accelerate the drafting phase. The research — checking sources, cross-referencing, confirming methodology behind statistics — still takes the same amount of time it always did. The people who get burned are the ones who skip that step because the AI output already looked finished.

**Know what AI is good at and where it breaks.** Great for structuring arguments, drafting narratives, brainstorming frameworks, summarising documents you've already read, and generating starting points. Unreliable for specific figures, recent data, niche domain facts, and anything where being wrong has consequences. The higher the stakes, the more human verification the output needs.

**What you can do now.** Document what happened honestly — what you used, where the data came from, what the failure point was. Not to punish yourself, but because that post-mortem is genuinely valuable. Plenty of people have made this exact mistake and will continue making it. You now understand something about AI tooling at a visceral level that most people only understand theoretically.

Losing a job is brutal. But the lesson here — that AI assists your judgment rather than replacing it — will serve you in every role after this one. The people who learn this the hard way tend to never make the mistake again.

What field were you working in? There might be specific tools or verification workflows I can point you toward so the next project has proper guardrails.
