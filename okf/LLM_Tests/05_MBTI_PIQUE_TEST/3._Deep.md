Quantum computing poses both a significant threat and an opportunity for cybersecurity. Here’s a breakdown of the key implications:

---

### 1. **Breaking Current Public-Key Cryptography**
- **Shor’s algorithm** (if run on a sufficiently large fault-tolerant quantum computer) can efficiently factor large integers and compute discrete logarithms.  
- This would break **RSA**, **Diffie-Hellman**, and **Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC)** — the backbone of secure internet communications (HTTPS, digital signatures, VPNs, etc.).  
- A practical quantum computer of that scale is still years away (estimates range from 5–20+ years), but the risk is that adversaries can **harvest encrypted data today** to decrypt later (“harvest now, decrypt later”).

---

### 2. **Weakening Symmetric Cryptography**
- **Grover’s algorithm** provides a quadratic speedup for brute‑force search.  
- To maintain the same security level, symmetric key lengths (e.g., AES) should roughly **double** (e.g., AES‑128 → AES‑256).  
- Hash functions (e.g., SHA‑256) also need larger output sizes to resist quantum collision attacks.  
- This is manageable through straightforward parameter upgrades.

---

### 3. **Post‑Quantum Cryptography (PQC)**
- New public‑key algorithms resistant to quantum attacks are being standardized.  
- **NIST** selected several algorithms (CRYSTALS‑Kyber for key exchange, CRYSTALS‑Dilithium for signatures, etc.) in 2022–2024.  
- **Migration** is a major challenge: updating protocols, hardware, and software across the entire digital infrastructure will take years.  
- Organizations are encouraged to begin **inventorying crypto usage** and planning for hybrid (classical + PQC) deployments.

---

### 4. **Quantum Key Distribution (QKD)**
- Uses quantum mechanics to create theoretically unbreakable symmetric keys.  
- Practical limitations: hardware cost, distance constraints, and the need for dedicated infrastructure.  
- Not a replacement for all cryptography, but a complementary tool for high‑security links.

---

### 5. **Timeline & Strategic Implications**
- **No immediate disruption** – current quantum computers are too small and error‑prone.  
- **Long‑term risk** demands proactive planning. Governments and standards bodies (NIST, ETSI) are pushing for PQC migration by the 2030s.  
- **“Crypto‑agility”** becomes critical: systems must be able to swap cryptographic algorithms without major redesign.

---

### 6. **What to Do Now**
- **Inventory** all uses of public‑key cryptography in your organization.  
- **Prioritize** long‑lived data (medical records, state secrets, financial archives) that could be decrypted in the future.  
- **Follow** NIST’s PQC standardization and begin testing hybrid implementations.  
- **Stay flexible** – the threat is evolving, but the foundational step is building the ability to upgrade crypto quickly.

---

In short, quantum computing will eventually force a fundamental shift in how we secure digital communications. While the end of RSA is not imminent, the transition to quantum‑safe cryptography is already underway—and delaying preparation creates significant long‑term risk.
