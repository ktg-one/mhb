The implications break down into three critical categories: immediate threats from "harvest now, decrypt later" attacks, the mathematical breaking of current encryption standards, and the massive migration challenge to post-quantum cryptography. **Immediate Threat: Harvest Now, Decrypt Later** Even before cryptographically relevant quantum computers (CRQCs) exist, adversaries—particularly nation-states—are intercepting and storing encrypted data today with the intent to decrypt it once quantum capability arrives . This transforms quantum computing from a future technical risk into an immediate intelligence vulnerability. Data with long-term value (financial records, health data, classified communications, infrastructure designs) is already being compromised in anticipation of future decryption . The Global Risk Institute's 2026 timeline estimates CRQCs are "quite possible" within 10 years and "likely" within 15 . **Mathematical Breaking of Current Encryption** Quantum computers will defeat current public-key cryptography through Shor's algorithm, which efficiently solves integer factorization and discrete logarithm problems—the mathematical foundations of RSA and ECC (Elliptic Curve Cryptography) . These algorithms protect secure web browsing, digital signatures, authentication protocols, and supply chain data exchange . Current quantum computers operate with ~1,000 qubits; breaking RSA-2048 requires approximately 1 million qubits . While this gap is significant, the trajectory is clear. Grover's algorithm presents a secondary threat to symmetric encryption (AES) by effectively halving key lengths, requiring larger keys and more computational resources to maintain equivalent security . **The Post-Quantum Migration Challenge** NIST finalized three post-quantum cryptography standards in August 2024: FIPS 203 (ML-KEM for key encapsulation), FIPS 204 (ML-DSA for digital signatures), and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA for hash-based signatures) . In March 2025, NIST selected HQC as an additional key encapsulation mechanism . These lattice-based and hash-based algorithms rely on mathematical problems believed resistant to both classical and quantum attacks . However, implementation is not a simple replacement. PQC algorithms introduce performance tradeoffs: increased latency, larger bandwidth requirements, and compatibility challenges with legacy systems . Google has set a 2029 deadline for full PQC migration, urging industry-wide adoption . Most governments target full quantum resistance by 2035 . **Strategic Implications** 1. **Cryptographic Agility**: Organizations must build infrastructure capable of switching algorithms without system replacement, as future cryptanalytic advances may compromise currently standardized schemes . 2. **Hybrid Approaches**: Industry is deploying hybrid algorithms (combining classical ECC with PQC like X25519MLKEM768) during transition phases. While NIST hasn't standardized hybrids, they allow FIPS 140 compliance if one component is approved . 3. **Ecosystem Vulnerability**: The threat extends beyond direct decryption to supply chain compromise, IoT devices with 10+ year lifecycles, and cloud infrastructure protecting data at rest and in transit . 4. **Geopolitical Dimension**: China is positioned as a frontrunner in quantum network infrastructure, while Western governments explicitly recommend PQC over Quantum Key Distribution (QKD) for most applications . **Recommended Actions** Organizations should immediately conduct cryptographic inventories to identify RSA/ECC dependencies, prioritize high-value assets with long confidentiality requirements, prototype PQC implementations to assess performance impacts, and design for cryptographic agility . The migration is estimated as a $15 billion global undertaking . The quantum threat is retroactive—unlike Y2K, the damage from data stolen today manifests years later.
