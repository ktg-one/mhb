Depends entirely on which mistakes they make. But here's the most probable path given those numbers.

**The money runs out faster than they think.** $200K across three founders is roughly 8-10 months of runway if they're all drawing even modest salaries in Perth. If they're not paying themselves, maybe 14-16 months on infrastructure, tooling, legal, accounting, insurance, and the random costs that multiply once you're actually operating. Either way, they're fundraising again by month 10 or they're profitable — and B2B SaaS for dentists is not profitable at 12 users.

**The 12 beta users are the entire business.** That number either triples in six months or the idea is dead. The question is whether those 12 are paying, how much, and what churn looks like. If they're free beta users "giving feedback," that's not validation — that's people being polite. If even 3 of them churn before launch, the founders will start second-guessing the whole thesis.

**Perth is both an advantage and a ceiling.** There are roughly 1,500-1,800 dental practices in WA. That's a small but tight market where word of mouth travels fast and you can literally drive to your customers. Great for early traction. Terrible for a venture-scale story. By month 12 an investor is going to ask "what's the national play" and the answer better not be "we'll figure it out."

**The founder dynamic will crack.** Three founders on $200K means someone feels underpaid, someone feels like they're doing more work, and someone starts questioning the equity split. This is almost guaranteed by month 9-12, especially if growth is slow. The ones who survive this have the hard conversations early. Most don't.

**The most likely 18-month outcomes, ranked by probability:**

One — they've got 40-80 paying customers in WA, modest MRR, haven't raised again, one founder has quietly stepped back or gone part-time. It's a real business but not a startup. This is the most common outcome and honestly not a bad one.

Two — they raised a small bridge round off the back of WA traction, are attempting east coast expansion, burning faster, and about to discover that selling to dentists in Sydney is a completely different motion than selling to dentists in Perth where you knew someone who knew someone.

Three — dead by month 14. The beta users didn't convert, the founders disagreed on pivot vs. persist, the money ran out, and the LinkedIn posts about "what I learned from my failed startup" are being drafted.

Four — the long shot — one of those 12 beta users is connected to a dental group or DSO, and suddenly they've got a channel partner handing them 30 practices at once. This changes everything but it's not plannable, it's luck meeting preparation.

The variable that determines which path they land on is almost never the product. It's founder alignment, speed of iteration on feedback from those 12 users, and whether they have the stomach to charge real money before the product feels ready.
